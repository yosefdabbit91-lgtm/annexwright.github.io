ISO/IEC 42001 gap assessment: what it actually does

A gap assessment is not an audit and it is not a certificate. It is a structured comparison between what the standard requires and what your organization currently has, producing a prioritised list of what is missing. This page sets out what to expect, what "readiness" honestly means, and where a workbook helps.

Written by Yosef Dabbit, Annexwright · Published 2026-09-23 · Last reviewed 2026-09-23 · Edition 2026.1

What it is

ISO/IEC 42001 splits into two things you can be measured against: the management-system clauses, which cover context, leadership, planning, support, operation, performance evaluation and improvement; and Annex A, the control set. A gap assessment walks both, asks "do we have this, and can we show it?", and records the answer with a justification.

The output is not a score for its own sake. It is a work list: what is missing, what exists but is undocumented, what is documented but not operating, and roughly what each one costs to close.

What it is not

  • Not an audit. An audit is performed against evidence by someone independent, to a defined programme. A gap assessment is preparation.
  • Not certification. Certification is issued by an accredited body after a two-stage audit. No assessment, workbook or document set produces a certificate.
  • Not a one-off. The first run tells you where you are. The value comes from re-running it after remediation and seeing the list shrink.

What "readiness" honestly means

A readiness percentage is a useful management number and a poor promise. It reflects how much of the required documentation and control coverage is in place — it cannot reflect the records that only elapsed time produces. You can be documentation-complete and still not be auditable, because a certification audit expects to see a completed internal audit, a management review that actually happened, and corrective actions with dates against them.

A realistic reading: documentation coverage tells you whether you are ready to start operating the management system. Time and records tell you whether you are ready to be audited.

How a gap assessment is usually run

  1. Scope. Which legal entity, which products, which AI systems. Scope decisions drive everything after them.
  2. Inventory first. You cannot assess what you have not listed. Most first-run gap assessments stall here, because AI features inside SaaS tools and models called by one team were never registered.
  3. Clause walk. Requirement by requirement: present, partial or absent, with a note on the evidence.
  4. Control walk. The Annex A control set, with an applicability decision and a justification for each.
  5. Prioritise. Sequence by dependency, not by how easy each item looks. Policy and inventory come before risk treatment, because the later work references them.
  6. Re-run. After remediation, to produce a trend rather than a snapshot.

Where a workbook helps

The mechanical parts — holding 65 requirements consistently, scoring them the same way twice, rolling them into a readiness figure, and turning the result into a sequenced roadmap — are exactly what a spreadsheet does well and what a document does badly. What a workbook cannot do is make the judgement calls: scope, applicability, risk appetite and whether a control you operate genuinely satisfies the requirement.
